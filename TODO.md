## Step 4: Career histories from the PhD to today (follow-up requested by the user on 17 October 2026)

Start once every person in the people database has a first-job review, and run it together with the graduate rosters of Step 2. Goal: for every operations PhD graduate, the full employment history from the degree to the latest observation, so that the site can show what happened right after the PhD and 5, 10 and 20 years later, and so that moves into and out of academia can be studied.

Why the current list cannot answer this on its own: everyone in the database today was found on a faculty list (OM List or a university faculty page), so the list contains only people who are in academia now (or retired from it). Graduates whose first job was in industry and who stayed there, and people who left academia later, are missing. The published data already shows that the opposite path exists: on 17 October 2026, 63 of the 1,398 verified first jobs were outside academia (50 industry, 11 government, 2 nonprofit), and all 63 people are now on a university faculty list (for example Foster Provost, NYNEX then NYU; Katya Scheinberg, IBM Research then Georgia Tech; Dashun Wang, IBM then Northwestern; Bruce Ankenman, Lubrizol then Northwestern). So "people who start in industry never come back" is an assumption to test, not a rule, and how common each path is can only be measured on graduate lists, not on faculty lists.

## Known pitfalls to retain

- The publish workflow failed on every push from about 07:14 UTC on 17 October 2026 until about 17:15 UTC, so the live site kept the morning's data while the repository moved on: `scripts/build-research-database.py` inserted placements for faculty-page people whose person rows it never created (only `data/faculty.json` was read), and SQLite rejected the foreign key. The exporter now reads `data/faculty-roster-people.json` too, and both merge routines run the schema test and the database build before committing. After any push to `main`, check the "Publish website" run and the live data file; a push alone does not show that the site updated.
- Parallel agents also share one scratch directory: on 17 October 2026 a second-pass researcher wrote helper scripts into shared scratch folders (`scripts/`, `recs/`) and may have overwritten another agent's files, and agents twice left downloads in the repository root (one held a person's email address and was moved out, not committed). The workflow briefs now require every helper and intermediate file in the agent's own scratch folder; the merge routine stages only named data files, so strays cannot be committed by it, but check `git status` before any manual commit.
- Parallel research agents share one web-search budget per session. Ten agents at once exhausted it, so most shard A chunks stopped after 7 to 30 people. Run fewer agents per session, or give each a smaller chunk, and let agents leave unreached people out of their proposal rather than writing empty reviews.
- Common Crawl's data server refuses bursts of range requests (HTTP 503) beyond a few in parallel; the scan uses two paced readers (six made it refuse most chunks), backs off and retries refused chunks slowly, and `scanned.json` lists any chunk still missing, so a crawl's hits can be incomplete; rerun `scan --crawl ID` after deleting its entry to read it again. Common Crawl captures a sample of each site per crawl, so a page can be missing from most crawls; scanning more crawls per year finds more captures.
- Many university sites (HBS, MSU, UCL, Cardiff, Technion and others) block automated fetches with 403 or bot pages. Unresolved reasons record those failed attempts; a human browser may resolve several quickly.
